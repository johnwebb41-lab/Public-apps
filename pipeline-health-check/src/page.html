A revenue operations command view, built end to end with AI

Built by John Webb

This is a working demonstration of how a B2B SaaS sales organisation can see the whole quarter in one place: where it will land, how much to trust that, which deals, reps and managers need attention, and what to do next. Every number is calculated live by a tested engine, and every recommendation comes from the figures, not from hand-written commentary.

None of this is real. The company, its customers, reps, managers, SDRs, competitors, deals and every figure were invented for this demo. Any resemblance to real organisations, people or results is purely coincidental.

It walks through each tab and explains why it's there.
1

Clarity on what matters

Revenue leaders ask the same questions in the same order: will we land, can we trust the pipeline, where are the weak spots, and what do we do about them. The demo is organised around those questions, with every formula defined and every assumption labelled.

2

AI that builds the whole system

The data model, calculation engine, automated tests, reviews and interface were all built with an AI model, directed by a RevOps practitioner. That's the point: AI can produce a joined-up system that holds the full picture, not just a single report.

3

Judgement, not just numbers

The engine spots issues, estimates where the quarter and year will land, scores how far to trust that, and ranks where to focus: which deals, which reps, which managers need help, and why.

What I decided, and why

John Webb
  1. Answer first, evidence second. The first screen tells a revenue leader where we'll land, how far to trust it and what to decide this week. Every other tab is the evidence behind that.
  2. Coverage against what's left, at our own win rate. Coverage against full quota flatters a late quarter. I measure it against quota still to find, and set the target from our own win rate rather than a blanket 3x.
  3. Show the pipeline with and without its problems. Raw coverage beside clean coverage puts the case for pipeline hygiene in one number.
  4. No red flag without a next step. A flag on its own is noise. Every issue comes with the action that fixes it, ranked by what it's worth.
  5. Calibrate before you forecast. Forecast categories are checked against history. Commit has closed at 80% here, not the 85% people assume, so that's the rate the forecast uses.
  6. Judge people against their tenure. A rep six months in at 66% is doing fine; one three years in at 71% isn't. Managers get flagged on facts, and told why.
  7. Commission is seen only by those entitled to it. Executives see every team; a team lead sees only their own. It appears nowhere else on the page.
  8. Nothing goes red without a reason. Every status says what triggered it, and every threshold is written under its table.
  9. Safe to share. All the data is invented, competitors are anonymised, nothing comes from any employer, and it was checked for legal risk before publishing.
  10. AI builds; people decide. AI wrote the code and played the reviewer roles. I decided what to measure, what good looks like, who sees what, and what goes in front of an executive.

    How I built it

    1. Define. I set out what each measure means and the order a leader should read them in, before anything was built.
    2. Build. I had the AI write a calculation engine of plain, testable functions, fed by invented data that comes out the same every time.
    3. Test. automated tests: worked examples, edge cases, and checks that every total agrees across tabs.
    4. Review. I had the work reviewed for method, wording and legal risk, and fixed what came back. The full process is under How it works.

    How to use it

    • The assumptions at the top (quota, closed won, win rate, stale days) drive every tab. Change one and everything recalculates.
    • Green, amber and red mean the same everywhere: Good Watch Act. The words change to suit the measure (On track, Healthy, Ahead), and a line under each table says exactly where the thresholds sit.
    • The tabs stay pinned to the top as you scroll. Hover the ? beside any measure for a one-line definition; click it for the full entry.
    • The Show buttons under the tabs filter every rated row and tile on a tab: Good, Watch, Act, or the watch list (Watch and Act together).
    • Wide tables keep their first column pinned as you scroll sideways.
    • Your data runs the same checks on a CSV export from your CRM, privately in the browser.

      How it works

      Three layers, one source of truth

      1. Fictitious data. All of it is generated from fixed seeds, so the same numbers appear every time.
      2. Calculation engine. Plain, separately tested functions for hygiene, coverage, landing, confidence, commission, marketing, renewals, capacity and attribution. The page only displays what the engine returns, so the tabs can't contradict each other.
      3. Judgement layer. Rules that turn figures into findings: which deals need a next step, which teams need support, which decisions leadership should take this week. Each rule is written down below, so every recommendation can be traced to a number.

      Read the tabs in order: Executive summary first for the answer, then the tabs behind it for the evidence. The assumptions at the top of the page feed every tab.

      How I built it, and how I'd do it for real

      Directed by a RevOps practitioner, built with AI

      1. Start from the decision, not the data. I began with the questions a CRO asks at quarter end and worked back to the measures that answer them.
      2. Define before building. Every measure was written down with its formula and threshold first, so the build had a specification to meet.
      3. Keep the maths apart from the page. One calculation engine feeds every tab, so two tabs can't disagree.
      4. Make the test data realistic. A demo that's all red teaches nothing. The pipeline is mostly tidy, with the kind of problems real teams have.
      1. Test everything, then reconcile. Each calculation has worked examples, and team, rep and company totals are checked against each other.
      2. Review three ways. Method by a RevOps expert role, wording by an editor role, and a legal check before anything was shared.
      3. Iterate on what users actually see. I reviewed each version as an executive would and sent back what wasn't clear, right down to box alignment.
      4. Check it in a real browser. Every tab was rendered on desktop and phone, in light and dark, before release.

      On a live project I'd add three things: a direct CRM connection in place of the CSV import, with permissions enforced at sign-in; thresholds and forecast rates agreed with Finance and sales leadership, not rules of thumb; and a named owner for every action the page recommends.

      To be clear about roles: the reviewers and developer were one AI model working through those roles at my direction. I made the decisions; the AI wrote the code.

      Reading the colours
      • Green, amber and red mean the same on every tab; the words change to suit the measure (On track, Healthy, Ahead).
      • Every red or amber says why: a reason line on each metric tile, a "Why" column for teams, the points taken off each deal's health score, and a line under each table giving the exact thresholds.
      • Thresholds are common SaaS rules of thumb or stated demo settings, never presented as industry law.
      Who sees what
      • Executives see every team, manager and rep.
      • Team leads see commission for their own team and reps only, on the Commission tab and in the scenario planner. Commission appears nowhere else.
      • In this demo the "Viewing as" switch stands in for sign-in. In a live system, role permissions in the CRM or BI tool would enforce it.
      The professor's three rules
      • Trust the data, then the coverage, then the trend. Dirty pipeline inflates coverage, and inflated coverage hides a weak forecast.
      • Calibrate before you forecast. Use the rates at which your own deals have actually closed, not rules of thumb.
      • Define every number, label every assumption. If a figure can't be explained in one line, it shouldn't be on a leadership page.
      What review changed
      • Coverage is measured against quota still to find, with the target set by win rate rather than a fixed 3x.
      • Coverage is shown with and without deals that have issues; the gap is the case for pipeline hygiene.
      • Forecast rates are calibrated from history: Commit closes at 80%, not the 85% assumed.
      • Testing caught an empty pipeline scoring 44/100 confidence, a CSV without a next-step column flagging every deal, and health deductions that didn't add up to the score. All fixed.
      • Velocity no longer gets a "Good" with no benchmark behind it, and commission is visible only to the people entitled to see it.
      • A legal check replaced competitor names that belonged to real companies with Rival A, B and C, and confirmed nothing comes from any real employer.
      • Stated limitations: single-touch source attribution, assumed renewal rates by health band, and commission estimates that treat each deal on its own.
      Formula reference (plain-English versions are on the Definitions tab)
      Executive summary
      Landing range
      Floor: closed + clean Commit × 85%. Likely: closed + Σ amount × category rate with Commit 85%, Best Case 45%, Pipeline 15%, and flagged deals halved. Stretch: closed + all Commit + all Best Case + Pipeline × 15%. Built so floor ≤ likely ≤ stretch always holds.
      Pipeline confidence
      Weighted score out of 100: clean coverage against what's needed (30), in-quarter value with no hygiene issues (20), Commit and Best Case value with full MEDDPICC (20), forecast accuracy over the last four quarters (15), and the share of quota still to find already sitting in clean Commit (15). 75+ is high, 55+ medium.
      Team flags
      A team is flagged for: likely landing under 90%, clean coverage under what's needed, fewer than 60% of deals clean, or two or more reps behind for tenure. One flag is "watch"; three or more is "needs support".
      Tenure
      Expected attainment rises in a straight line from 40% at hire to 100% at 12 months. A rep is ahead at 110% or more of that expectation, behind under 90%. Ramp is the first nine months.
      Marketing
      Funnel
      MQL: marketing qualified lead. SAL: accepted and worked by an SDR. SQL: qualified by the SDR and handed to an AE. Opportunity: accepted by the AE. Rates are from the previous stage.
      Dumped and recirculated
      Dumped = disqualified, with a reason, and not coming back. Recirculated = recycled to nurture, to re-qualify later.
      Where marketing pipeline stands
      Every opportunity that started as an MQL: landed (won), likely (open Commit or Best Case, no hygiene issue), may not land (Pipeline category or any hygiene issue), lost. Uses the same rules as the landing forecast.
      Conversion of decided leads
      SQLs ÷ (SQLs + disqualified + recycled). Leaves out leads still being worked, which would otherwise drag the rate down.
      Pipeline per £1
      pipeline from a channel's MQLs ÷ channel spend. Single-touch: each deal is credited to one source.
      Activity, renewals and planning
      Activity
      Calls, emails and meetings logged in the last 30 days. Meetings a week = meetings ÷ (30 ÷ 7). Connect rate = conversations ÷ calls; show rate = held ÷ booked.
      Account health
      Starts at 100 and loses points: usage down over 20% (−30) or 5–20% (−15), each priority-1 ticket (−8, up to −24), no engaged executive sponsor (−15), NPS below 0 (−15) or below 30 (−5), late payment (−10), champion left (−20). 70+ is healthy, 45+ at risk, below that critical. Forecast renewal uses 97%, 80% and 50% by band (assumed).
      Scenario
      Deals marked won count in full and pay commission on top of each rep's closed business; deals marked lost drop out; pulled deals move into Q4 at their normal category rate. Everything else uses the calibrated landing rates.
      Capacity
      Σ months (annual quota ÷ 12 × ramp × productivity × survival). Ramp is 0% for months 0–2, 33% for 3–5, 66% for 6–8, then 100%. Survival applies annual attrition month by month. Productivity defaults to the average trailing attainment of reps with 12+ months' tenure.
      Win/loss and attribution
      Calibrated category rates
      For historical deals that reached Solution, the share that closed won from each forecast category they sat in. These replace the assumed 85/45/15 in every landing figure.
      Head-to-head win rate
      Won ÷ closed for deals where that competitor was involved. Small samples swing; check the deal count.
      Attribution models
      First touch: all credit to the first channel. Last touch: all to the converting MQL. Linear: equal shares. Position-based: 40% first, 40% last, 20% shared across the middle. Every model credits the same total.
      Your data
      CSV import
      Runs the same hygiene rules, next steps, coverage and landing calculations on your file, in the browser. Checks that need a column you didn't provide are skipped and listed, rather than failed.
      Coverage
      Quota remaining
      quota − closed won. Coverage is measured against what is left to find.
      Raw, clean and weighted coverage
      in-quarter pipeline ÷ quota remaining; clean counts only deals with no hygiene issue; weighted multiplies by stage probability (target 1.0x).
      Coverage needed
      1 ÷ win rate. The "3x rule" is this with a 33% win rate baked in. The win rate box defaults to the TTM win rate from the metrics tab.
      Next steps and commission
      Next steps
      One play per hygiene issue, one per MEDDPICC element below the stage minimum, and one for any discount or terms awaiting sign-off. Ranked by priority × deal value.
      Commission views
      Executives see every team; a team lead sees only their own team and reps, on the Commission tab and in the scenario planner. Commission is not shown anywhere else. In a live system this would be enforced by sign-in and role permissions.
      Manager override
      2% × team closed won; expected override uses the team's probability-weighted Q4 bookings.
      Commission if won
      base slice × rate + slice above quota × rate × 1.5, assuming this deal alone closes on top of what the rep has closed. Expected = that × stage probability. Potential assumes every in-quarter deal closes in close-date order.
      Retention
      GRR
      (ARR a year ago − churn − contraction over the year) ÷ ARR a year ago, capped at 100%.
      NRR
      (ARR a year ago + expansion − churn − contraction) ÷ ARR a year ago. Includes expansion from customers won during the year, a common simplification that flatters NRR slightly.
      Expansion velocity
      Expansion ARR per month (TTM), expansion as a share of the starting base, and quarter-on-quarter change in expansion bookings.
      Renewal forecast accuracy
      1 − |renewed − forecast| ÷ renewed.
      Pipeline
      Pipeline velocity
      qualified open deals × average deal × win rate ÷ cycle days. The four levers are the four things a sales leader can change.
      Stage conversion
      Deals that reached the next stage ÷ deals that reached this one.
      Win rate
      Won ÷ (won + lost), only deals that reached Qualification. Counting Discovery drop-outs as losses would understate it badly.
      Cycle length
      Median days from creation to won. Median, because a few marathon deals drag the mean.
      Forecasting and efficiency
      Forecast accuracy and bias
      Accuracy 1 − |actual − call| ÷ actual. Bias (call − actual) ÷ actual: persistently negative means sandbagging, positive means happy ears.
      CAC payback (months)
      last quarter's S&M ÷ (net new ARR × gross margin ÷ 12).
      Net magic number
      net new ARR ÷ last quarter's S&M. Above 0.75 says spend more; below 0.5 says fix the engine first.
      Rule of 40
      revenue growth YoY + free cash flow margin.
      Projection
      Trend line
      Least-squares straight line through the quarters shown, projected four quarters on. The shaded band is a 95% prediction interval; it widens because the further out you go, the less the past constrains the future. A straight line is a sense check, not a forecast: it ignores seasonality and anything you plan to change.
